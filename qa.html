Terminology and Diagnosis

What is the difference between psychopathy and Antisocial Personality Disorder (ASPD) as described in the DSM-5, and how should the two terms be used in academic research?

by Robert Hare · reply to a reader, 2026

Popular culture and the media often use terms such as "psychopath," "sociopath," and "antisocial personality" rather loosely, sometimes as though they were interchangeable. They are not.

The first point I would emphasize is that psychopathy and Antisocial Personality Disorder (ASPD) are related constructs, but they should not be regarded as synonymous.

Psychopathy, as I have used the term in my research, is a multidimensional clinical construct involving a constellation of interpersonal, affective, lifestyle, and antisocial characteristics. The interpersonal and affective components include such features as superficial charm, grandiosity, deception and manipulation, shallow affect, lack of empathy, lack of remorse or guilt, and failure to accept responsibility. The lifestyle and antisocial components include impulsivity, irresponsibility, poor behavioral controls, stimulation seeking, and persistent antisocial behavior.

ASPD, as defined in Section II of the DSM-5, places considerably greater emphasis on observable antisocial and socially deviant behavior. Although there is overlap with psychopathy—for example, in deceitfulness, impulsivity, irresponsibility, aggression, and lack of remorse—the DSM criteria do not require many of the interpersonal and affective characteristics that have traditionally been regarded as central to psychopathy.

This produces what we have often described as an asymmetrical relationship between the two constructs. In offender populations, most individuals with very high levels of psychopathy will also satisfy diagnostic criteria for ASPD. The reverse, however, is not true: many individuals who meet the criteria for ASPD do not have the interpersonal and affective characteristics associated with psychopathy.

This distinction is very important for academic research. I would advise against using "psychopathy" and "ASPD" interchangeably. If you are discussing ASPD, make clear that you are referring to the DSM construct and its diagnostic criteria. If you are discussing psychopathy, state clearly how you are defining and operationalizing the construct—for example, in relation to the clinical tradition represented by Cleckley and the Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R) framework.

There is an additional complication in the DSM-5. Section III, which presents an alternative model for personality disorders, moves somewhat closer to the psychopathy construct by including traits such as manipulativeness, deceitfulness and callousness, and it also contains a psychopathy specifier for ASPD. This should not, however, be taken to mean that psychopathy and the conventional Section II diagnosis of ASPD have become the same construct.

For research on media representations, this distinction is especially useful. Criminality, violence, cruelty, or violation of social norms by themselves do not establish psychopathy. Psychopathy involves a broader configuration of personality traits and behaviors, and antisocial behavior need not necessarily be criminal. Similarly, the presence of charm, confidence, emotional detachment, or manipulativeness alone is not sufficient to identify someone as psychopathic.

I have repeatedly cautioned against casual or "long-distance" diagnosis. A label such as psychopath has considerable power and should not be applied simply because an individual—or a fictional character—appears violent, remorseless, charismatic, manipulative, or frightening. A proper clinical assessment requires the accumulation of information across different domains and, in the case of instruments such as the PCL-R, appropriate professional training and collateral information.

This caution provides an important framework for analyzing how the media romanticize psychopathy. Films and other media can select particularly dramatic aspects of psychopathy—charm, apparent emotional coolness, manipulation, violence, or social dominance—while neglecting the complete pattern of personality and behavior. The result can be a highly attractive, mysterious, or almost superhuman caricature rather than a representation of the clinical construct.

For this reason, rather than asking only whether a particular character "is a psychopath," one might ask which features associated with psychopathy are portrayed, which are omitted, exaggerated, or romanticized, and whether the portrayal is being confused with ASPD, criminality, psychosis, or other forms of psychopathology. Methodologically and conceptually, that would be a much more defensible approach.

Assessment, the PCL-R, and Misuse

If psychopathy is a valid clinical construct, how should we think about the line between defining and measuring it and the harm that can follow when institutions apply the label or the assessment carelessly or coercively?

by Robert Hare · reply to a reader, 2026

The distinction between attempting to define and measure a clinically important construct and what institutions subsequently do with that knowledge is an important one. It is also something that has concerned me for many years.

Psychopathy is not a term that should be applied casually. Nor should an instrument such as the PCL-R be treated as an oracle. It is an assessment procedure intended to organize information about a constellation of interpersonal, affective, lifestyle, and antisocial characteristics. Properly used, it requires training, considerable information about the individual, and careful professional judgment. A score does not relieve the clinician—or the institution employing the clinician—of responsibility for what is subsequently done.

Indeed, one of the dangers of psychiatric and psychological labels is that they can acquire a life of their own. Once entered into a file, a diagnosis may influence people who know little about how it was obtained, its limitations, or the degree of uncertainty surrounding it. The authority of the label can then become greater than the quality of the evidence that produced it.

Scientific or clinical language does not confer ethical legitimacy upon an intervention. One may have a defensible construct and still have an indefensible application of it. The questions of informed consent, coercion, proportionality, professional accountability, and institutional oversight remain quite separate from whether a particular psychological theory or assessment instrument has empirical merit.

Psychology and psychiatry have not always been sufficiently modest about this distinction. There is an understandable temptation to move from "we have evidence that this characteristic exists" to "we therefore know what should be done with the person who possesses it." That second proposition requires a very different kind of justification.

My own concern has generally been to make the assessment of psychopathy more explicit, reliable, and empirically accountable than the impressionistic diagnoses that preceded it. But no improvement in measurement eliminates the possibility of misuse. A good instrument in poorly trained, ideologically committed, or institutionally powerful hands can still produce harmful consequences.
